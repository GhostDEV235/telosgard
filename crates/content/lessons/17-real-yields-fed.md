[[steps]]
heading = "Gold's hidden cost"
text = "Gold pays no interest. Holding it means giving up the income you could earn elsewhere, for example from a safe US government bond. That missed income is gold's **opportunity cost**. The bigger it is, the less attractive gold tends to look."
why = "The World Gold Council lists opportunity cost, the US dollar and bond yields together, as one of gold's main drivers. You met the dollar half in Lesson 16; this lesson is the yields half."

[[steps]]
heading = "Real yield: interest after inflation"
text = "A bond's **real yield** is roughly its interest rate minus expected inflation. A bond paying 4.5% with 2.5% inflation expected has a real yield of about **2%**. Traders watch the **10-year TIPS yield**, the US government's inflation-protected bond, as the measure."
why = "TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) are adjusted for inflation, so their yield is already a real yield. The Federal Reserve publishes it daily; on FRED it's the series DFII10."

[[steps]]
heading = "Real yields up, gold usually down"
text = "When real yields **rise**, the safe bond pays more after inflation, so holding gold costs more in missed income: gold **usually falls**. When real yields **fall**, gold's opportunity cost shrinks and gold **usually rises**. Usually, not always."
why = "The World Gold Council expects rising yields and a stronger dollar to pull money out of gold funds, and lower rates to help. But it is one driver among several: in some periods other forces, such as fear or central-bank buying, have kept gold rising while real yields were high."

[[steps]]
heading = "Where the Fed comes in"
text = "The **Federal Reserve** sets the US short-term interest rate eight times a year at **FOMC** meetings. Its decisions, and its hints about what comes next, shift what markets expect for all rates, and so real yields. That's why gold often moves sharply on FOMC days."
why = "Markets trade expectations. If everyone already expects a rate rise, the rise itself may barely move gold; a surprise, or a change in the Fed's tone about the future, is what moves it. Lesson 19 is about exactly that."
